What six years inside a health district taught me about data.

I joined Galveston County Health District in late 2019 as a data specialist. What I walked into was not one data problem. It was all three of the problems that show up in every under-resourced public health environment. Data existed but nobody could access or trust it. Leadership was making decisions without it. And what did get collected rarely turned into anything useful. At different points over six years, I worked through all of it.

The moment I understood why I was doing this work happened not in a data review or a pipeline deployment. It happened watching someone outside the data team, a program director, a department head, someone who had never looked at a dashboard before, suddenly understand something about their own operation that they had not been able to see. That moment, where data crosses the gap between existing and being useful, is the thing I have spent my career trying to engineer.

Most organizations have more data than they can act on. The failure is rarely in collection. It is in the gap between data existing and data meaning something to the person who needs to make a decision. I have built pipelines, dashboards, and predictive tools, but all of it has been in service of closing that gap. The technical work is the means. The decision is the point.

I started as a physiotherapist, which means I have always understood that the person on the other end of the analysis is a patient, a community, a population with real consequences attached. That sensibility does not go away when you move into data. It changes how you decide what to build, how you frame a finding, and who you design for. I am looking for environments where that orientation is an asset, where healthcare data is treated as a serious infrastructure problem, and where there is room to use AI thoughtfully rather than just quickly.
